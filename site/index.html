1What's a coda

Sperm whales make some of the loudest sounds of any animal. When they meet, they trade short rhythmic bursts of clicks. A burst is called a coda.

Codas are learned, not inherited — whales in different clans use different rhythms. That's why they matter: this is one of the strongest cases of culture in a non-human animal.

Three words you need.

Coda — the whole burst.  ·  Click — one pulse inside it.  ·  ICI — the gap between one click and the next (inter-click interval).

What we don't know: whether codas mean anything. Nobody has shown that. Anyone who says we've decoded whale language is selling something.

2Clicks and gaps

A coda is defined by when each click happens. Everything else is derived from that.

Try it. Listen, then click the spectrogram where each click starts. You'll be scored against the published answer.

Two rules of the pipeline.

Clicks separated by more than 0.55 s belong to different codas. And a run of more than 10 clicks is rejected outright — the published Dominica repertoire runs 1 to 10 per coda, so a longer run is two codas merged, or an echolocation train.

The scorer on this page is simpler than the pipeline: a mark counts if it lands within 30 ms of a published click. That's it.

Clicks look like vertical stripes. Ignore the faint horizontal haze — that's background noise. If you place two marks within about 12 ms of each other the tool treats that as a misclick and removes one. Sperm whale clicks do have a multi-pulse structure, but the detector handles that — you just mark the click.

3Names are a trap

Researchers name codas by their rhythm. The notation looks like 1+1+3 or 5R1: groups of clicks, and R for a run of evenly spaced ones.

Here's the problem. Two published schemes name the same coda differently, and a third numbers them.

Three shapes worth recognising

All three are real codas, all recorded by a tag on the whale. Listen to how different the rhythms are — that difference is what the names describe.

What the notation actually encodes. In the Dominica scheme a label packs several things into one string: a click count, R for a run of regular clicks, + for grouped clicks, letters like D for a descending pattern, i for something else again.

Read the table, not the names. A label packs a click count and a spacing pattern into one string, and the encoding is not obvious: three labels here describe codas whose real difference is how far apart the clicks are. The name is a compressed description, not something you can read off a rulebook — which is why different labs compress differently, and why we store times instead.

So our pipeline never names a coda. It records click times. Names get derived later, under a scheme we declare. That is the single most important design decision in the repo — if we baked a name in, our data would stop being comparable with anyone else's.

4The data, and the trick

Public coda data is two halves that don't fit together: 1,501 audio clips (45 minutes) with no labels, and 8,719 measurements in a spreadsheet with no audio.

I assumed we'd have to label the audio ourselves. We didn't. Each public clip turned out to be one published coda with exactly 1.000 s of padding (half a second at each end) — so the label is hiding in the file length.

Published labels here are treated as the answer. They are assumed correct — this project has not independently re-verified them.

The consequence.

That corpus leaks its own labels. Any result that treats those clips as unlabelled audio is invalid, and random train/test splits don't fix it. But it's also a gift: 1,382 clips with known answers — a free benchmark.

5Where the machine breaks

Same task, two recordings. Listen and watch: the left is a hydrophone suction-cupped to the whale. The right is a hydrophone towed behind a boat in shallow water.

In shallow water each click hits the surface and the seabed and comes back. Those echoes look exactly like extra clicks to a simple detector.

That single fact decides whether the pipeline works:

Pooled, that's 19% — a number that describes neither half. When you see a single accuracy figure from me, ask which rig it's from.

6When is a label trustworthy

Give the same clip to two people and their clicks won't land in identical places. The question is whether the label survives that wobble.

Numbers are the share of re-reads that keep the rhythm within 15%.

The slider is a simulation, not a measurement — it re-reads the rhythm under synthetic jitter. No human annotators have marked this corpus yet, so no real agreement figure exists.

This is inter-annotator agreement — IAA. Without it, “we labelled 500 codas” has no floor under it. With it, you can say “two people found the same clicks to within 7 ms” and a reader can decide whether to believe you.

Note what the slider shows: agreement depends on how fast the whale was clicking. Any agreement figure that doesn't say which codas it covers is close to meaningless.

7Reviewing my work

Five questions. If a claim fails any of them, it isn't ready.

  • 1
    Does a script print this number? Every figure in our docs should come out of a command you can run. If I quote something I measured once and never scripted, that's a defect.
  • 2
    Is it split by recording rig? Towed and whale-borne are different problems. A pooled accuracy number is hiding the only thing that matters.
  • 3
    Does agreement say which codas? Fast codas are fragile, slow ones aren't. One IAA number across both is an average of two different things.
  • 4
    Are we pretending the corpus is unlabelled? It isn't. Any evaluation on the public clips must account for the leak.
  • 5
    Is a single number standing in for a distribution? “Median 6 clicks” can be true while every clip is wrong. Ask for the per-clip error, not the summary.
What a bad answer looks like

“The detector is 95% accurate on codas.” No rig, no per-clip error, no script, measured on a corpus that leaks its labels. Every one of those is a hole a reviewer should push on — and I will have made some of these mistakes. That's what this page is for.
